7. Statistical and conceptual concerns

Means, medians and unusual weeks

A mean affected by an unusual observation is not automatically a bad statistic. The full mean accurately summarises recorded output across the entire supplied period. Removing week 7 produces a different, labelled population of eleven weeks; it does not discover the true output of an otherwise identical twelve-week period. The activity recognises that exclusion can mislead and recommends reporting both figures. That is sound. The problem is the median definition, not its calculation. “Unusual” or “stands out” is justified descriptively for week 7; no formal outlier test or licence to discard it has been established or is necessary for this lesson.

Dates, exposure and different periods

The dataset supports comparisons across the specified weeks but contains no calendar dates, production hours, staffing levels, product mix or run-time denominator. The late period has five weeks and the early period seven. The app correctly teaches that more units after overtime does not prove greater units per hour. Downtime hours themselves are measured, but a downtime rate or reliability measure would also need a defined exposure period. No long-term improvement should be inferred from the three above-target observations.

Scrap denominator and precision

There is no need to discard the parts exercise. It is useful if explicitly framed as an estimate under a visible assumption. The source does not say whether scrap is counted by pieces, mass, value, input quantity or another denominator. It also does not say whether units produced is a gross count or a good-output count. The app eventually acknowledges some of this uncertainty but initially defines the percentage as a share of produced units and uses factual verbs about scrap counts.

Assumption A: units produced is the denominator
  Estimated scrap = units produced × p
  W7: 3,650 × 0.039 = 142.35 → about 142
  W12: 5,150 × 0.026 = 133.90 → about 134
  W7 rate / W12 rate = 1.5
  Estimated count ratio = 142.35 / 133.90 ≈ 1.0631
  Estimated difference = 8.45 parts (8 between displayed rounded counts)

Alternative hypothetical definition, not a source fact:
  G is good output and p is scrap / gross output
  Scrap = G × p / (1 − p)
  W7: 3,650 × 0.039 / 0.961 ≈ 148.13
  W12: 5,150 × 0.026 / 0.974 ≈ 137.47

Under that particular alternative, the discrepancy is only a few parts. It does not follow that every possible real definition will differ only slightly. The figures support a conditional teaching calculation, not a verified physical stock count.

Sensitivity example only, if rates were rounded to nearest 0.1 percentage point:
  W11 reported 2.3%: an underlying rate from 2.25% up to 2.35%
  Conditional count range: 5,080 × [0.0225, 0.0235) = [114.30, 119.38)
  W5 reported 2.4%: an underlying rate from 2.35% up to 2.45%
  Conditional count range: 4,780 × [0.0235, 0.0245) = [112.33, 117.11)
  The intervals overlap. Actual ordering need not match 117 versus 115.

This does not alter the authoritative 2.3% and 2.4% inputs, which remain the correct values to use for the exercise. It shows why a two-part estimated difference should not be described as a verified ordering of actual scrap counts. Exact piece counts require clarification of both denominator and precision.

Reporting changes and causation

Recorded calls fall before the form changes as well as after it: 20 in week 8, 16 in week 9, then 15, 14 and 13. The form cannot simply be assumed to explain the whole fall, and the app does not explicitly make that claim. It correctly asks whether the recording process is comparable. Likewise, Saturday overtime and higher output coincide but do not establish improved efficiency. The storm is established contextual evidence, but full attribution of production loss and other measures is not. The week 7 feedback should use the same caution the later screen already uses.

DATA-001 • Scrap estimates are presented as physical counts before their assumption is explained

Severity: High

Location: ST[7], lines 1338–1380; MEAS.scrap and MEAS.sparts, lines 475–479; ST[8] table captions, lines 1456–1464.

Actual behaviour: The introduction says “A percentage is always a share of whatever was produced that week”. Selecting any week immediately equates its recorded percentage with units produced and shows an approximate parts figure. The denominator warning appears only after both weeks 7 and 12 have been selected. The later narrative says week 12 “still scrapped nearly as much stock” and that week 11 scrapped more parts than week 5. “Approximate” does not explain the denominator assumption.

Expected behaviour: A percentage is a share of a specified total. The brief does not define that total. The multiplication is a valid illustrative estimate if units produced is the denominator. Actual scrap counts and their ordering are not established by the supplied figures alone.

Evidence: All 12 multiplications and rounded outputs match the independent reference. Generated HTML for the first selection, week 1, shows 101 parts without the denominator panel. That panel is present after weeks 7 and 12 have both been selected. Week 11 gives 5,080 × 0.023 = 116.84 and week 5 gives 4,780 × 0.024 = 114.72. These are conditional estimates, not verified counts. If the percentages themselves were rounded to the nearest 0.1 percentage point, the possible ranges overlap; that is a sensitivity example, not an assumption about how the source was prepared.

Why it matters: This is a central lesson about questioning figures, but its headline calculation initially assumes the missing definition. The small 117 versus 115 comparison can be mistaken for a proven reversal in measured scrap counts.

Recommended correction: Keep the activity. Put the assumption above the charts from the first render: “For this example, assume the scrap percentage is a share of units produced. Estimated scrap parts = units produced × scrap % ÷ 100. The sheet does not define the denominator, so these are not confirmed scrap counts.” Change the comparison to “Using that assumption, week 11 gives an estimate of about 117 parts and week 5 about 115. Ask for the actual counts before concluding which scrapped more.” Use “Estimated scrap parts” consistently in charts and tables. Remove the unqualified claim that the actual figures must differ only slightly.

Confidence: High. Arithmetic and conditional output directly executed; source definition checked against both supplied references.

DATA-002 • The median is described as an actual middle week

Severity: Medium

Location: ST[5], line 1208; spoken feedback, line 1222; ST[9], lines 1540–1542.

Actual behaviour: The screen says “A typical week, meaning the middle week of the twelve, produced 4,920.” The spoken feedback and printout also label 4,920 as a typical week without identifying it as the median.

Expected behaviour: The median is 4,920, calculated from the two middle values after sorting production. No supplied week produced 4,920. “Middle week” can also be misread as chronological position.

Evidence: Sorted positions 6 and 7 are 4,910 and 4,930. Their mean is (4,910 + 4,930) ÷ 2 = 4,920. The actual chronological weeks 6 and 7 produced 4,990 and 3,650.

Why it matters: The numeric result is correct, but the sentence teaches the wrong definition of a median for an even-sized dataset and implies an observation that does not exist.

Recommended correction: Use: “The median is 4,920 units. Put all 12 production figures in order; the two middle values are 4,910 and 4,930. Halfway between them is 4,920.” On the printout use “median 4,920 across all 12 weeks”. Retain the mean labels and the clear explanation of the 11-week subset.

Confidence: High. Independent Python calculation, JavaScript constants and both rendered mean states agree.

DATA-003 • The printout attaches the week 7 decision to a potentially different week

Severity: Medium

Location: ST[2] decision at lines 954–957; ST[9] selected-week and answer block, lines 1526–1532.

Actual behaviour: The learner may select any of weeks 1–7 for investigation, but the later yes/no question always asks about week 7. The final output prints the selected week and then says “I would still raise it” or “I was satisfied it was explained” without naming week 7.

Expected behaviour: The output should record the selected investigation week separately from the learner’s decision about week 7.

Evidence: Executed selection week 5 plus “no” generates: Week 5: 4,780 units, 16 hours of downtime, 25 fault calls, 2.4% scrap. After reading the note about the storm, I was satisfied it was explained. Selecting week 1 plus “yes” has the same referent problem. All 14 week/answer combinations were generated; the six non-7 selections are affected for either answer.

Why it matters: A portable workplace note can imply the storm explains week 5, or claim the learner decided to raise a different week. The selected numerical row itself is correct.

Recommended correction: Use separate labels: “Week I selected for investigation: Week …” and “My decision about week 7 after reading the storm note: I would still ask about week 7” or “I chose not to raise week 7 after reading the note.” Do not derive the decision’s week from S.s2.week.

Confidence: High. Actual ST[9] render output checked for all seven weeks and both answers.

DATA-004 • A supplied note example says all week 7 measures were below the others

Severity: Medium

Location: BLANKS[0].starters, line 1411; carried through ST[8] and ST[9].

Actual behaviour: The app offers “week 7 was well below every other week on every measure” as a ready-made observation and can place that sentence into the final note.

Expected behaviour: Week 7 had the lowest production, but the highest downtime, fault calls and scrap percentage. If “below” means poorer performance, that needs to be stated clearly rather than sounding like lower numerical values.

Evidence: Week 7: 3,650 units; 38 hours downtime; 41 calls; 3.9% scrap. All other weeks have higher units and lower values in the remaining three columns. The prewritten sentence was passed through the actual final render and reproduced unchanged.

Why it matters: The app supplies a misleading observation for a learner who may depend on its examples to describe chart direction correctly.

Recommended correction: Replace it with: “week 7 had the lowest production and the highest downtime, fault calls and scrap percentage”. This is correct for both weeks 1–7 and all 12 weeks.

Confidence: High. Full-source comparisons and generated final note verified.

DATA-005 • A downward arrow marks unchanged downtime in week 12

Severity: Medium

Location: TREND.dt, line 1242; ST[6] dataTable trend annotations.

Actual behaviour: Every downtime cell from week 10 onwards gets a downward arrow, including 8 hours in week 11 and 8 hours in week 12. The arrows have no stated comparison baseline.

Expected behaviour: If arrows mean change from the previous week, week 12 should be marked unchanged or have no arrow. If they mean comparison with week 8, that reference must be explicit.

Evidence: TREND.dt uses n >= 10 rather than comparing values. The executed table contains week 11 “8 ▼” and week 12 “8 ▼”. Independent change: 8 − 8 = 0 hours.

Why it matters: A directional mark next to each row suggests a further reduction that did not occur. The separate statement that downtime fell from week 8 to week 12 remains correct.

Recommended correction: Smallest correction: omit the week 12 downtime arrow. Better local rule: compare wk(n).dt with wk(n−1).dt and return down, up or blank, with a caption “Arrows compare with the previous week”. Apply the same stated reference consistently to the other columns.

Confidence: High for generated annotation; actual pixel rendering not tested.

DATA-006 • The storm note is treated as a complete explanation of missing production

Severity: Medium

Location: ST[2], lines 956 and 961–963; associated final wording in ST[9].

Actual behaviour: Both decisions receive the explanation “The note explains why the parts were not produced”. The answer label “No, the note explains it” also implies closure.

Expected behaviour: A power interruption is relevant context and a plausible contributor to lower output. The brief does not quantify production lost to the outage, explain all 38 downtime hours, or attribute all changes in calls and scrap to the storm.

Evidence: Week 7’s shortfall is 5,000 − 3,650 = 1,350 units, but there is no outage duration, output rate, cause breakdown or counterfactual production figure. The only source note says severe storm and power lost for part of the week.

Why it matters: The activity correctly teaches caution about overtime and reporting changes later, but overstates causal certainty at this earlier decision.

Recommended correction: Use: “The power loss helps explain why production may have been lower. The sheet does not show how much of the shortfall it caused, or whether it explains the other changes.” A suitable answer label is “No, I would not raise it for now”. Keep both learner choices acceptable.

Confidence: High that full attribution is not established; the storm being a relevant explanation is not disputed.

DATA-007 • The rank explanation says being worst does not distinguish first worst from second worst

Severity: Low

Location: ST[2] takeaway, lines 942–945.

Actual behaviour: The text says “being worst on all four is not what separates week 7 from week 5”. The adjacent table correctly ranks week 7 worst and week 5 second worst in every measure.

Expected behaviour: Their ranks do distinguish them. The larger instructional point is that ranks alone do not show how much more extreme week 7 is.

Evidence: Worst-to-best rankings across weeks 1–7 begin [7, 5] for all four measures. Production gaps of 40 units from week 1 to week 5 and 1,130 units from week 5 to week 7 are both correct.

Why it matters: A useful lesson about magnitude is expressed through a sentence that contradicts its own table.

Recommended correction: Use: “Weeks 7 and 5 are the bottom two on all four measures, but the ranks do not show the size of the gap. Week 5 produced 40 fewer units than week 1; week 7 produced 1,130 fewer than week 5.”

Confidence: High. All four ranking functions compared with the independent source.

DATA-008 • Fictional example data is described as a real quarter

Severity: Low

Location: ST[0], line 718.

Actual behaviour: The welcome text says “This activity uses a real quarter from that sheet”.

Expected behaviour: The candidate brief identifies Ridgeway Components as fictional. It supplies 12 weeks but no dates or claim that this is a genuine operational quarter.

Evidence: The brief’s Context section explicitly calls Ridgeway Components a fictional manufacturer. No supporting real-world provenance is provided.

Why it matters: This is minor scenario wording, but it gives the dataset authenticity the evidence does not establish. Twelve weeks can be a teaching period without claiming an exact calendar quarter.

Recommended correction: Use: “This activity uses 12 weeks of example data from that sheet.” The Monday meeting setting can remain as ordinary fictional framing.

Confidence: High. Direct comparison with the candidate brief.

  1. Make the scrap assumption visible before any estimate. Keep the multiplication and all source rates unchanged. Label all resulting counts as estimates and make the two comparisons conditional. Apply the same label in the note’s reference tables.

  4. Replace the misleading suggested note. State that week 7 had the lowest production and highest values in the three other measures.
  5. Fix the week 12 downtime annotation. Remove its down-arrow or calculate arrows against a clearly stated reference with a neutral result for ties.
  6. Qualify the storm explanation. Say it helps explain the result without assigning the entire shortfall or every measure change to it.
  7. Apply the two low-severity wording corrections. Explain that ranks do not show magnitude, and call the dataset 12 weeks of example data.
